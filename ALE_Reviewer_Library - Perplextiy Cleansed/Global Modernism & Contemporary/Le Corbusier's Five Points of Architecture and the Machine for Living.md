## Core Concepts
- **Le Corbusier's Five Points of Architecture**: A set of architectural principles introduced by Swiss-French architect Le Corbusier.
  - Pilotis (columns)
  - Free plan 
  - Ribbon windows
  - Roof garden
  - Free façade

- **The Machine for Living**: An idea that buildings should be designed with the same efficiency and rationality as machines, emphasizing functionality over ornamentation.

## Additional Details

Le Corbusier, a pioneering Swiss-French architect, urban planner, designer, painter, writer, and journalist, introduced his Five Points of Architecture in 1926 as part of his vision for modern architecture. These principles were intended to address the needs of contemporary living by creating efficient spaces that could be adapted easily over time.

### Le Corbusier's Five Points of Architecture
Le Corbusier’s five points are:
1. **Pilotis**: The use of reinforced concrete columns (pilotis) to raise buildings above ground level, freeing up space below for various uses.
2. **Free Plan**: Interior walls should not be load-bearing; instead, they can be placed freely within the building's structure, allowing flexibility in layout and design.
3. **Horizontal Windows**: Long horizontal windows provide even lighting throughout a room while maintaining privacy through vertical blinds or curtains.
4. **Roof Garden**: The roof of buildings is transformed into green spaces to compensate for lost ground-level gardens due to elevated structures on pilotis.
5. **Free Façade**: Exterior walls are independent from the building’s structure, allowing them to be designed without concern for internal layout.

### The Machine for Living
The concept of "the machine for living" (la maison-souche) was a central theme in Le Corbusier's work and philosophy. He envisioned houses as machines that could adapt to changing needs over time, much like how automobiles evolve with technological advancements. This idea emphasized the importance of functional design, efficiency, and flexibility within architectural spaces.

These principles were revolutionary at the time and have since influenced modernist architecture globally, though their direct application in specific regulations or codes such as those found in PD 1096 RIRR 2004 is not evident from the provided sources.
