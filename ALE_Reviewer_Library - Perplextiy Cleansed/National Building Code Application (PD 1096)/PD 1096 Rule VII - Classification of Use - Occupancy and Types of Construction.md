# Architecture Board Exam (ALE) Mastery: Occupancy Groups & Construction Types

**Module Focus: PD 1096 — Classification by Use/Occupancy (Chapter 7 / Rule VII) & Types of Construction (Chapter 4 / Section 401)**

---

## The "Why It Matters" Scenario (Schema Activation)

Imagine you are designing a sleek, 30-storey BPO office tower in Makati, and for your next project, you design a small wooden beach house in La Union. The materials, fire escapes, and structural limits for these two buildings are worlds apart.

Why? Because the National Building Code of the Philippines (PD 1096) mandates that **what happens inside the building (Occupancy)** dictates **what the building must be made of (Construction Type)**.

> **WARNING TO ALE TAKERS:** A massive trap is studying from foreign textbooks (like US IBC reviewers) instead of the Philippine Code. The Philippine system uses the **same Roman numerals (I–V)** as the American IBC but **in the exact opposite order** of fire resistance. This module is strictly aligned with PD 1096 — forget everything from American codes.

---

## Core Concepts (The "Building DNA")

### 1. Classification of Use/Occupancy (Groups A to J)

- **Legal Basis:** PD 1096, Chapter 7, Section 701 (codified in Rule VII of the Revised IRR)
- **Definition:** The Code categorizes every building based on its primary function. Different functions carry different fire risks, crowd densities, and evacuation needs.
- **10 Groups, 25 Divisions:** There are 10 Groups of Occupancies sub-divided into 25 Divisions.
- **Reason for the Law:** The government needs to apply different safety standards to different uses. A hospital with bedridden patients needs stricter exit requirements than a warehouse. Classifying by use allows the Code to calibrate life-safety requirements precisely.

### 2. The "Mixed Occupancy" Rule (Section 703)

> **STOP — This is a common exam trap.** The review material you may have seen elsewhere says "the building must adhere to the rules of the most restrictive occupancy group." That is only the general rule. Section 703 has **two critical exceptions:**
>
> **(a)** When a **one-storey** building houses more than one occupancy, **each portion** of the building conforms to the requirements of the particular occupancy housed therein.
>
> **(b)** Where minor accessory uses do **not** occupy more than **10%** of any floor area or 10% of the basic area permitted, the **major use** determines the occupancy classification.
>
> **Reason for the Law:** A small first-aid clinic inside a shopping mall should not force the entire mall to be classified as a hospital. The exceptions prevent absurd over-regulation while still protecting life safety.

### 3. Types of Construction (Types I to V)

- **Legal Basis:** PD 1096, Chapter 4, Section 401 (NOT Section 703 — Section 703 is Mixed Occupancy)
- **Definition:** The Code grades the physical structure of the building based on the combustibility and fire-resistance rating of its materials.
- **The Rule of Progression (PH Edition):** In the Philippines, the scale goes from the **weakest/most combustible (Type I = Wood)** to the **strongest/most fire-resistive (Type V = Fire-Resistive)**.
- **Reason for the Law:** Fire is the single greatest mass-casualty threat to building occupants. Grading materials by fire resistance allows the Code to mandate stronger construction for higher-risk occupancies (hospitals, high-rises) while permitting lighter materials for low-risk uses (farm sheds).

### Types of Construction — The Fire Resistance Scale

> **PATTERN INTERRUPT — Burn this into your brain:**
>
> | PH Code (PD 1096)                       | US IBC Code                             |
> | :-------------------------------------- | :-------------------------------------- |
> | Type I = **WOOD** (Weakest)             | Type I = **Fire-Resistive** (Strongest) |
> | Type V = **FIRE-RESISTIVE** (Strongest) | Type V = **Wood** (Weakest)             |
>
> **They are EXACT OPPOSITES. The board will test this.**

| Type         | Material / Combustibility (Exact Code Language)                                                                                                                            | Fire-Resistance Requirement                                          | Example Application                                                                                         |
| :----------- | :------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------------- | :---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Type I**   | **Wood Construction.** Structural elements may be any materials permitted by the Code.                                                                                     | **None specified** (most combustible)                                | Traditional wooden stilt houses (bahay kubo), temporary sheds, nipa huts.                                   |
| **Type II**  | **Wood Construction with Protective Fire-Resistant Materials.** One-hour fire-resistive throughout. Non-bearing partitions may use fire-retardant treated wood.            | **1-Hour Fire-Resistive throughout**                                 | Treated wood structures, wood-framed buildings with fire-retardant cladding.                                |
| **Type III** | **Masonry and Wood Construction.** One-hour fire-resistive throughout. **Exterior walls shall be of incombustible fire-resistive construction.**                           | **1-Hour Fire-Resistive throughout; Exterior walls = incombustible** | CHB exterior walls with wooden interior framing (common ancestral homes, older schools).                    |
| **Type IV**  | **Steel, Iron, Concrete, or Masonry Construction.** Walls, ceilings, and permanent partitions = incombustible. Non-bearing partitions may use fire-retardant treated wood. | **Incombustible construction** (materials do not burn)               | Modern commercial buildings, steel-framed warehouses, mid-rise offices.                                     |
| **Type V**   | **Fire-Resistive.** Structural elements = steel, iron, concrete, or masonry. Walls, ceilings, and permanent partitions = incombustible fire-resistive construction.        | **Fire-Resistive** (highest grade)                                   | High-rise towers, hospitals, government buildings. Structural elements encased in concrete or fireproofing. |

**Reason for the Law:** The progression from Type I to V reflects increasing protection of human life. A wooden beach hut can burn down with minimal casualties; a 30-storey hospital cannot be allowed to collapse. The Code forces builders to use stronger, fire-resistant materials as the risk to occupants increases.

> **KEY DISTINCTION (Types II vs III):** Both are "1-hour fire-resistive throughout." The difference is that Type III **mandates incombustible exterior walls** (masonry/concrete), while Type II allows wood framing for exterior walls (with fire-retardant treatment). This is a classic board exam trap.

> **KEY DISTINCTION (Types IV vs V):** Both use incombustible materials. The difference is that Type V is **explicitly fire-resistive** — meaning the structural elements are designed and tested to maintain integrity under fire for extended periods, not merely built from materials that do not burn. Type IV is incombustible but may lack the tested fire-resistance duration required of Type V.

## High-Yield Exam Traps (Von Restorff Effect)

Your brain is fighting against foreign media and architectural textbooks. Here is how board examiners will try to trick you:

**TRAP 1: The "US IBC" Hallucination**

- _Mistake:_ Thinking Group B is Business, Group M is Mercantile, and Group A is Assembly.
- _Reality:_ That is the American IBC. In PD 1096: **Group B = Hotels/Apartments**, **Group E = Business/Mercantile**, **Group H/I = Assembly**. There is no Group M in the Philippine code.

**TRAP 2: Reversing the Construction Types**

- _Mistake:_ Thinking Type I is Fire-Resistive and Type V is Wood (the US IBC order).
- _Reality:_ In PD 1096, **Type I is Wood (weakest)** and **Type V is Fire-Resistive (strongest)**. Mnemonic: _"1 is flimsy, 5 is a fortress."_

**TRAP 3: Group A vs. Group B Confusion**

- _Mistake:_ Putting an apartment building in Group A.
- _Reality:_ Group A is for single-family dwellings and non-leasing occupants **not exceeding 10 persons** (Division A-2). Once it becomes a multi-family apartment, hotel, or boarding house accommodating **more than 10 persons**, it becomes **Group B**.

**TRAP 4: Group A Is Not Just "Houses"**

- _Mistake:_ Thinking Group A is only for residential houses.
- _Reality:_ Group A Division 1 also includes **churches**, church rectories, community facilities, social centers, parks, playgrounds, and membership clubhouses (not operated primarily for gain). Group A Division 2 includes **schools up to 16 classrooms**, on-campus dormitories, convents, outpatient clinics, and branch libraries. Know your Divisions.

**TRAP 5: Section 703 Is NOT About Construction Types**

- _Mistake:_ Citing "Section 703" as the legal basis for Types of Construction.
- _Reality:_ Section 703 is about **Mixed Occupancy** rules. Types of Construction are in **Chapter 4, Section 401** of PD 1096. The board will test whether you know which section governs what.

**TRAP 6: Group H vs Group I — The Stage Requirement**

- _Mistake:_ Thinking any assembly with 1,000+ people is automatically Group I.
- _Reality:_ Group I specifically requires an assembly building **with a stage** and an occupant load of **1,000 or more**. Group H Division 1 is also with a stage but with occupant load **less than 1,000**. Group H Division 2 is **without** a stage but with 300+ occupant load.

## Active Recall Challenge (Test Your Retention)

### Question 1

An architect is designing a 5-storey shopping mall in Cebu City with a reinforced concrete structure and a fire-resistive rating. Under PD 1096, what are the correct Occupancy Group and Construction Type?

A) Group M (Mercantile), Type I
B) Group E (Business and Mercantile), Type V
C) Group C (Commercial), Type IV
D) Group E (Business and Mercantile), Type I

**Answer: B) Group E (Business and Mercantile), Type V**

**Why?**
Step 1: A shopping mall is a retail/business occupancy = **Group E** (not Group M or Group C — those do not exist in PD 1096).
Step 2: Reinforced concrete with fire-resistive rating = **Type V** (the highest grade in the Philippine scale; Type I in PD 1096 is wood).

---

### Question 2

A convent accommodates 12 nuns. Under the 2004 Revised IRR of PD 1096, what occupancy group does this building fall under?

A) Group A, Division 1
B) Group A, Division 2
C) Group B
D) Group D, Division 1

**Answer: C) Group B**

**Why?**
Group A Division 2 covers convents and monasteries for **non-leasing occupants not exceeding 10 persons**. Group B covers convents, monasteries, and similar buildings accommodating **more than 10 persons**. Since 12 > 10, this convent is **Group B**.

---

### Question 3

What is the key difference between Type II and Type III construction under PD 1096?

A) Type II uses masonry; Type III uses wood.
B) Both are 1-hour fire-resistive, but Type III requires incombustible exterior walls.
C) Type II is incombustible; Type III allows wood framing.
D) Type III has a 2-hour fire-resistive rating; Type II has 1-hour.

**Answer: B) Both are 1-hour fire-resistive, but Type III requires incombustible exterior walls.**

**Why?**
Per Section 401: Type II = wood construction, 1-hour fire-resistive throughout. Type III = masonry and wood construction, 1-hour fire-resistive throughout, **and exterior walls shall be of incombustible fire-resistive construction.** The mandatory incombustible exterior is what separates them.
