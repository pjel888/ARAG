# ALE Mastery: Occupancy Groups & Types of Construction

**Module Focus: PD 1096 — Classification by Use/Occupancy (Chapter 7, Rule VII) & Types of Construction (Chapter 4, Section 401)**

**Legal Basis: Presidential Decree No. 1096 (National Building Code of the Philippines) and the 2004 Revised Implementing Rules and Regulations (IRR) as published by the DPWH**

---

## Schema Activation: Why This Module Decides Everything

You are reviewing two projects simultaneously. One is a 30-storey BPO office tower in Makati with a reinforced concrete superstructure and a fire-resistive rating. The other is a small wooden beach house in La Union. The materials, fire escapes, structural limits, and legal requirements for these two buildings are worlds apart.

Why? Because PD 1096 mandates that **what happens inside a building (Occupancy)** determines **what the building must be made of (Construction Type)**. Get the classification wrong, and every specification that follows — from exit widths to fire-resistance durations — is built on an incorrect premise.

> **BOARD EXAM WARNING — PATTERN INTERRUPT:**
> A large proportion of ALE failures in this module come from studying foreign code textbooks (US IBC reviewers) instead of PD 1096. The Philippine system uses the **same Roman numerals I through V** as the American IBC but **in the exact opposite order of fire resistance.** This module is strictly and exclusively aligned with PD 1096 and the 2004 Revised IRR. Discard every convention from American codes before proceeding.

## Core Legal Framework

### 1. Classification by Use/Occupancy (Groups A to J)

| Element | Detail |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Legal Basis** | PD 1096, Chapter 7, Section 701; Rule VII of the 2004 Revised IRR |
| **Definition** | Every building is categorized by its primary function. Different functions carry different fire risks, crowd densities, and evacuation demands. |
| **Scale** | 10 Groups (A through J), sub-divided into 25 Divisions |

**Reason for the Law:** The government cannot apply identical safety standards to a warehouse and a hospital. Classifying buildings by use allows the Code to calibrate exit widths, structural requirements, and sprinkler mandates precisely to the actual risk profile of each occupancy. One-size-fits-all regulation would either under-protect high-risk buildings or economically strangle low-risk ones.

---

### 2. Mixed Occupancy (Section 703)

> **PATTERN INTERRUPT — TRAP ALERT:**
> Most review materials state only the general rule: "the building must adhere to the rules of the most restrictive occupancy group." Section 703 contains **two critical exceptions** that the board routinely tests.

**General Rule:** A building with two or more occupancy groups follows the requirements of the most restrictive group.

**Exception (a):** In a **one-storey building** with multiple occupancies, **each portion** follows the requirements of its own particular occupancy — not the most restrictive one.

**Exception (b):** Where minor accessory uses occupy **not more than 10%** of any floor area (and not more than 10% of the basic area permitted), the **major use governs** the occupancy classification for the entire building.

**Reason for the Law:** A small first-aid clinic inside a shopping mall should not force the entire mall to meet hospital-grade standards. The exceptions prevent absurd over-regulation while still protecting life safety in the portions of the building where higher risk actually exists.

---

### 3. Types of Construction (Types I to V)

| Element | Detail |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Legal Basis** | PD 1096, **Chapter 4, Section 401** — NOT Section 703 |
| **Definition** | The Code grades buildings by the combustibility and fire-resistance rating of their structural materials |
| **PH Scale Direction** | **Type I = Weakest (Wood) → Type V = Strongest (Fire-Resistive)** |

**Reason for the Law:** Fire is the single greatest mass-casualty threat in buildings. Grading materials by fire resistance allows the Code to mandate stronger construction for higher-risk occupancies — hospitals, high-rises, assembly halls — while permitting lighter construction for low-risk uses such as agricultural sheds.

## The Hard Data: Types of Construction I through V

### The Philippine-vs-US Comparison Table

> **PATTERN INTERRUPT — BURN THIS INTO YOUR MEMORY:**
>
> | PD 1096 (Philippine Code) | US IBC |
> | :--- | :--- |
> | **Type I = WOOD** (Weakest, most combustible) | Type I = Fire-Resistive (Strongest) |
> | **Type II = Wood + 1-hour fire-resistive** | Type II = Non-combustible |
> | **Type III = Masonry + Wood, incombustible exterior** | Type III = Non-combustible, limited combustible |
> | **Type IV = Steel/Iron/Concrete/Masonry, incombustible** | Type IV = Heavy Timber |
> | **Type V = FIRE-RESISTIVE** (Strongest) | Type V = Wood (Weakest) |
>
> **They are EXACT OPPOSITES. The board will test this. There is no middle ground — you either know the Philippine scale or you answer from the wrong code.**

### Construction Types — Full Detail Table

| Type | Official Name | Material Requirements | Fire-Resistance Requirement | Typical Application |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Type I** | Wood Construction | Structural elements may be any materials permitted by the Code | **None specified** — most combustible type | Traditional wooden stilt houses, temporary sheds, bahay kubo |
| **Type II** | Wood Construction with Protective Fire-Resistant Materials | Wood framing; non-bearing partitions may use fire-retardant treated wood | **1-hour fire-resistive throughout** | Treated-wood structures with fire-retardant cladding |
| **Type III** | Masonry and Wood Construction | Wood interior framing; **exterior walls shall be of incombustible fire-resistive construction** | **1-hour fire-resistive throughout; exterior walls = incombustible** | CHB exterior walls with wood interior framing — common in older schools and ancestral homes |
| **Type IV** | Steel, Iron, Concrete, or Masonry Construction | Walls, ceilings, and permanent partitions = incombustible; non-bearing partitions may use fire-retardant treated wood | **Incombustible construction throughout** — materials do not burn, but fire-resistance duration is not the governing criterion | Mid-rise commercial buildings, steel-framed warehouses |
| **Type V** | Fire-Resistive Construction | Structural elements = steel, iron, concrete, or masonry; walls, ceilings, and permanent partitions = incombustible fire-resistive construction | **Full fire-resistive** — structural elements are designed and tested to maintain integrity under fire for a defined duration | High-rise towers, hospitals, government buildings with encased structural elements |

**Reason for the Law:** The progression from Type I to Type V reflects the increasing scale of potential casualties if a building fails under fire. A wooden beach hut can burn with minimal loss of life; a 30-storey hospital cannot be permitted to collapse. The Code forces builders to use stronger, more fire-resistant materials in direct proportion to the risk borne by occupants.

---

### Critical Distinctions: The Pairs the Board Tests

> **KEY DISTINCTION — Type II vs. Type III:**
> Both are "1-hour fire-resistive throughout." The sole difference: Type III **mandates incombustible exterior walls** (masonry or concrete). Type II permits wood exterior framing treated with fire retardant. If an exam question describes a building with concrete block exterior walls and wood interior framing — that is Type III, not Type II. This is a classic board trap.

> **KEY DISTINCTION — Type IV vs. Type V:**
> Both use incombustible materials throughout. The difference is the standard of performance: Type V structural elements are **explicitly fire-resistive** — they have been designed and tested to withstand fire for a defined period without losing structural integrity. Type IV is built from non-burning materials but may not be tested to a fire-resistance duration. Incombustible does not equal fire-resistive.

## High-Yield Exam Traps

### Trap 1 — The US IBC Hallucination

**Mistake:** Applying US IBC group letters — Group B for Business, Group M for Mercantile, Group A for Assembly.

**Reality:** In PD 1096:
- **Group B = Hotels and Apartments**
- **Group E = Business and Mercantile**
- **Group H / I = Assembly**
- There is no Group M in the Philippine Code

Every exam question that lists "Group A (Assembly)" or "Group M (Mercantile)" as a correct answer is a trap designed to catch students who studied the wrong code.

---

### Trap 2 — Reversing the Construction Type Scale

**Mistake:** Answering that Type I is the strongest (fire-resistive) construction type.

**Reality:** In PD 1096, the scale runs from weakest to strongest:

```
Type I   →  Type II  →  Type III  →  Type IV  →  Type V
Wood         Wood+        Masonry+     Incomb.      Fire-Res.
(Weakest)    1-hr FR      1-hr FR      Throughout   (Strongest)
```

Mnemonic: *"One is flimsy. Five is a fortress."*

---

### Trap 3 — Group A vs. Group B: The 10-Person Line

**Mistake:** Classifying an apartment building or a boarding house as Group A.

**Reality:** Group A Division 2 covers non-leasing residential occupancies **not exceeding 10 persons**. The moment a multi-family unit, apartment, hotel, or boarding house accommodates **more than 10 persons**, it becomes **Group B**. The number 10 is the hard legal threshold — not 9, not 11.

---

### Trap 4 — Group A Is More Than Houses

**Mistake:** Thinking Group A contains only single-family homes.

**Reality:**
- **Group A, Division 1** includes churches, church rectories, community facilities, social centers, parks, playgrounds, and membership clubhouses not operated primarily for gain.
- **Group A, Division 2** includes schools with **up to 16 classrooms**, on-campus dormitories, convents, outpatient clinics, and branch libraries — provided they are non-leasing and house no more than 10 persons.

Knowing the divisions is not optional. The board asks about specific building types, not the group label.

---

### Trap 5 — Section 703 Is NOT About Construction Types

**Mistake:** Citing "Section 703" as the legal basis for Types of Construction.

**Reality:**
- **Section 703** = Mixed Occupancy rules (general rule + two exceptions)
- **Chapter 4, Section 401** = Types of Construction (Types I through V)

These two topics live in entirely different chapters. The board tests whether you can cite the correct section, not just the correct answer.

---

### Trap 6 — Group H vs. Group I: Stage AND Headcount Both Required

**Mistake:** Assuming any building with 1,000 or more occupants is automatically Group I.

**Reality:** Group I requires a building **with a stage** AND an occupant load of **1,000 or more**. Both conditions are mandatory simultaneously.

| Scenario | Correct Group |
| :--- | :--- |
| With stage + less than 1,000 occupants | **Group H, Division 1** |
| Without stage + 300 or more occupants | **Group H, Division 2** |
| Without stage + less than 300 occupants | **Group H, Division 3** |
| With stage + 1,000 or more occupants | **Group I** |

A 1,500-seat open-air stadium without a stage is **Group H Division 4** — not Group I.

## Active Recall Challenge

<details>
  <summary><b>Question 1 — Occupancy and Construction Type Identification</b></summary>
  <br>
  An architect is designing a 5-storey shopping mall in Cebu City. The structure is reinforced concrete with a full fire-resistive rating. Under PD 1096, what are the correct Occupancy Group and Construction Type?
  <br><br>
  A) Group M (Mercantile), Type I<br>
  B) Group E (Business and Mercantile), Type V<br>
  C) Group C (Commercial), Type IV<br>
  D) Group E (Business and Mercantile), Type I<br>
  <br>
  <b>Correct Answer: B</b>
  <br><br>
  <b>Step 1 — Occupancy:</b> A shopping mall is a retail and business occupancy. In PD 1096, that is <b>Group E (Business and Mercantile)</b>. Group M does not exist in the Philippine Code. Group C is Education and Recreation.<br><br>
  <b>Step 2 — Construction Type:</b> Reinforced concrete with a fire-resistive rating is <b>Type V</b> — the strongest grade in the Philippine scale. Type I in PD 1096 is wood construction, not fire-resistive. Option A contains two traps simultaneously: a non-existent group letter AND the reversed construction type.
</details>

---

<details>
  <summary><b>Question 2 — The 10-Person Threshold</b></summary>
  <br>
  A convent accommodates 12 nuns who live there on a non-leasing basis. Under the 2004 Revised IRR of PD 1096, what occupancy group does this building fall under?
  <br><br>
  A) Group A, Division 1<br>
  B) Group A, Division 2<br>
  C) Group B<br>
  D) Group D, Division 1<br>
  <br>
  <b>Correct Answer: C</b>
  <br><br>
  Group A Division 2 covers convents and monasteries for non-leasing occupants <b>not exceeding 10 persons</b>. Group B covers convents, monasteries, and similar buildings accommodating <b>more than 10 persons</b>. Since 12 > 10, this convent is <b>Group B</b>.<br><br>
  Group D Division 1 would apply to hospitals or jails — populations with restricted liberty or acute medical dependency. Non-ambulatory patients housed in groups over 5 persons fall under D-2. Nuns living voluntarily in a convent do not meet that threshold.
</details>

---

<details>
  <summary><b>Question 3 — Type II vs. Type III Construction</b></summary>
  <br>
  What is the key difference between Type II and Type III construction under PD 1096?
  <br><br>
  A) Type II uses masonry; Type III uses wood.<br>
  B) Both are 1-hour fire-resistive, but Type III requires incombustible exterior walls.<br>
  C) Type II is incombustible throughout; Type III allows wood framing.<br>
  D) Type III has a 2-hour fire-resistive rating; Type II has 1-hour.<br>
  <br>
  <b>Correct Answer: B</b>
  <br><br>
  Per Section 401:<br>
  - <b>Type II</b> = Wood construction, 1-hour fire-resistive throughout. Exterior walls may be wood with fire-retardant treatment.<br>
  - <b>Type III</b> = Masonry and wood construction, 1-hour fire-resistive throughout, AND <b>exterior walls shall be of incombustible fire-resistive construction</b>.<br><br>
  The mandatory incombustible exterior wall is the only distinction. Both share the same 1-hour fire-resistive rating. Option D invents a 2-hour rating that does not exist for either type. Option A reverses the material descriptions.
</details>

---

<details>
  <summary><b>Question 4 — Mixed Occupancy Exception</b></summary>
  <br>
  A single-storey building contains a convenience store (Group E) on one side and a small barber shop (also Group E) on the other. There is no structural separation between them. Under Section 703, what rule governs?
  <br><br>
  A) The most restrictive occupancy governs the entire building.<br>
  B) Each portion follows its own occupancy requirements because it is a one-storey building.<br>
  C) The larger occupancy governs because it exceeds 10% of the floor area.<br>
  D) The building must be reclassified as Group H because of public foot traffic.<br>
  <br>
  <b>Correct Answer: B</b>
  <br><br>
  Section 703, Exception (a): When a <b>one-storey building</b> houses more than one occupancy, each portion of the building shall conform to the requirements of its own particular occupancy — not the most restrictive group. The "most restrictive governs" rule applies only to multi-storey mixed-occupancy buildings as the general default, subject to the exceptions.
</details>

```
Type I   = Wood — structural elements any permitted material; no fire-resistance specified
Type II  = Wood + 1-hour fire-resistive throughout; exterior walls may be treated wood

Type IV  = Steel / Iron / Concrete / Masonry; incombustible construction throughout
Type V   = Fire-Resistive — structural elements steel, iron, concrete, or masonry;
           walls, ceilings, partitions = incombustible fire-resistive; tested duration
```
